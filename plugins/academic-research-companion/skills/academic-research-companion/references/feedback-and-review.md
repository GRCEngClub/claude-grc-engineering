# Phase 5: Feedback & Peer Review

Goal: get the draft attacked by qualified readers before the world sees it, and handle formal review well when a venue is involved.

## The feedback ladder (climb it in order)

1. **Self-review with distance** — a week away from the draft, then read as a hostile reviewer. Offer to simulate this: critique the draft against the venue's likely review criteria (soundness, novelty, clarity, significance), flagging the three weakest points.
2. **Trusted peers** — the user's own network is unusually strong for this: a ~700-member GRC community, cross-agency AI Community of Practice contacts, and instructor connections. Ask 2–3 people with different lenses (one domain expert, one methods-minded reader, one intelligent outsider). Give each a specific job: "Is the method sound?" beats "thoughts?"
3. **Community pre-review** — post a preprint or working draft (OSF, SSRN, a working-paper section of the user's site) and solicit comments; present at a meetup, community call, or workshop work-in-progress session. Feedback here is free peer review.
4. **Formal peer review** — comes bundled with journal/conference submission (see `publishing.md`).

## Receiving feedback

- Collect everything before reacting; look for convergence — a point two independent readers raise is real regardless of whether the user agrees.
- Triage into: must-fix (soundness), should-fix (clarity/completeness), taste (optional).
- Confusion is data: if a smart reader misread a section, the section failed, not the reader.

## How formal peer review actually works (set expectations)

- **Models**: double-blind (neither side named — anonymize the manuscript), single-blind (reviewers see author), open review. Check the venue's model before formatting.
- **Timeline**: journals commonly take 2–6+ months per round; conferences run fixed review cycles tied to deadlines. Plan around it; polite status queries after the stated window are normal.
- **Outcomes**: accept (rare on first pass) / minor revisions / major revisions ("revise & resubmit" — this is a *good* outcome, most published papers pass through it) / reject. Rejection is routine, often fit-related, and usually comes with usable reviews. Revise and target the next venue.
- **Desk rejection**: editor bounces it without review, usually scope or format mismatch — a venue-selection error, not a quality verdict.

## Responding to reviewers (when revising)

Produce a point-by-point response document: quote each reviewer comment, state the change made and where, or give a respectful, evidence-based reason for not changing. Address every point; never ignore one. Tone: gracious even when a reviewer is wrong — "the reviewer raises an important point; we have clarified…" Reviewers are unpaid volunteers who gave the work hours; write like it.

## Reviewing others (fastest way to learn the genre)

Encourage the user to review: practitioner venues and workshops often welcome qualified practitioner reviewers, and community/club paper-reading sessions build the muscle. Reviewing teaches what reviewers punish — the single best writing education available. It's also service that builds standing in a field (and a line on the eventual academic CV).

## No affiliation? Not a blocker

Journals and conferences accept independent submissions; use "Independent Researcher" as the affiliation. What matters to reviewers is the manuscript. Where affiliation genuinely helps (IRB access, some grant-funded venues), collaboration is the tool — see `authorship-collaboration.md`.

## Exit criteria for Phase 5

- At least two qualified humans have read the draft and their feedback is triaged in the tracker
- Must-fix items resolved
- Decision recorded: publish independently as-is, or proceed to venue submission (Phase 6)
