# Phase 7: Authorship & Collaboration

Applies at any phase — when the user considers adding co-authors, or is invited onto someone else's project as a second/third author.

## What makes someone an author

The widely used baseline is the **ICMJE criteria** — an author should have (1) contributed substantially to conception/design or data/analysis, (2) drafted or critically revised the work, (3) approved the final version, and (4) agreed to be accountable for it. People who helped but don't meet the bar (gave feedback, provided data access, proofread) belong in the **Acknowledgments**, not the byline.

Two integrity violations to name plainly if they come up:
- **Gift/honorary authorship** — listing someone who didn't contribute (a boss, a friend, a big name for credibility)
- **Ghost authorship** — omitting someone who did contribute substantially

## CRediT: the modern way to divide labor

The **CRediT taxonomy** defines 14 contributor roles (Conceptualization, Methodology, Software, Validation, Formal analysis, Investigation, Resources, Data curation, Writing – original draft, Writing – review & editing, Visualization, Supervision, Project administration, Funding acquisition). Many venues now require a CRediT statement. Use it as a **planning tool at project start**: assign roles before work begins and author order arguments mostly evaporate.

## Author order

Conventions differ by field — check the target field's norms before assuming:
- **Most sciences/engineering/IS**: first author = did the most work; last author = senior/supervising; middle authors in descending contribution
- **Economics, mathematics, some theory fields**: alphabetical order, no hierarchy implied
- **Corresponding author** = handles submission and reader contact; can be any position

Decide order **early and in writing**, revisit if contributions shift, and resolve disputes against the CRediT record rather than memory.

## Bringing others onto the user's project (as 2nd/3rd authors)

Good reasons to add a co-author: a methods skill the user lacks (e.g., a statistician), domain depth, an institutional affiliation that unlocks IRB review or data access, or an arXiv endorsement path. Recruiting sources for an unaffiliated researcher: the user's professional community (GRC club members with academic ties), UMGC faculty (instructors are realistic collaborators for a strong graduate student — approach with a concrete one-page proposal, not a vague ask), and authors of closely related papers (a specific, well-informed email works more often than people expect).

Set terms at the start, in a short written note all parties keep:
1. Scope of each person's contribution (CRediT roles)
2. Provisional author order and the condition for changing it
3. Decision rights (who calls venue choice, what happens on disagreement)
4. Timeline expectations and what happens if someone goes dark (common failure — agree on a "step down to acknowledgment" clause)
5. Data/code ownership and reuse rights for follow-up work

This feels overly formal for a friendly collaboration; it is exactly what keeps friendly collaborations friendly.

## Joining someone else's project as 2nd/3rd author

A realistic and efficient way to gain peer-reviewed publications and learn the process from inside. Before saying yes, do diligence — the user's name means shared accountability for the whole paper:

- **Understand the full study**, not just the assigned slice. Ask to see the data, the analysis, the design. "I only did the software" does not insulate a co-author from a retraction.
- **Verify the lead is credible**: prior publications real? Target venue legitimate (run the predatory screen)? Ethics/IRB handled if human subjects?
- **Get the same written terms as above** — contribution scope, order, expectations — before starting work.
- **Contribute enough to defend the paper**: a good self-test is whether the user could present the paper's core argument and answer questions on it.

Red flags to decline: pay-for-authorship offers, "we just need your name," refusal to share data/methods with a co-author, target venue that fails the predatory screen.

Where to find these opportunities: the user's communities (GRC club, AI CoP), UMGC faculty projects, open calls for collaborators in research Slacks/Discords and academic Twitter/Mastodon/Bluesky, and offering a concrete skill (the user's automation/tooling capability is genuinely scarce in policy-adjacent research groups — "I can build the data pipeline" is a strong entry ticket).

## Working practices for a healthy collaboration

- Shared repository (Zotero group + Google Drive/GitHub) from day one
- Standing written status rhythm (even async, even brief)
- The manuscript has one owner per section at any time; comments over parallel edits
- Log major decisions where all authors see them — the tracker works for this; share it

## Exit criteria

- Every collaboration has: written terms, CRediT role assignments, provisional author order, and a decision log
- The tracker's collaboration section reflects current state
